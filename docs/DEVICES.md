# A second device, and why it joins the way it does

A person's conversations live on one device today. This is how a second one
joins them, what that costs everybody else in the conversation, and which of the
obvious alternatives are refused and why.

The crypto layer already holds the shape: `Member::for_device` makes a device a
leaf of its own, `Participant` carries which device a leaf is, and the safety
number is taken over the leaves so adding one moves it. What follows is the part
above that, which is where every decision worth arguing about lives.

---

## What is being protected against

A second device is an **addition to every conversation the person is in**. So
the question is not "how do I get my laptop working", it is:

> What stops somebody else's key package being added to all of Ana's
> conversations, and who finds out when one is?

Everything below is arranged around that sentence.

---

## The shape

1. The new device makes an identity of its own, `for_device(person, "laptop")`,
   and a key package from it. **Nothing is copied from the first device.**
2. That key package reaches the first device **by whichever route suits the two
   of them**. The routes are listed below and they are interchangeable.
3. The package is **authenticated**, by one of the two means below, and nothing
   proceeds until it has been. Which means depends on the route.
4. The first device then, for each conversation, commits an Add and sends the
   Welcome to the new device, under a tag both derive from the exchange.
5. Every partner receives that commit like any other. **Their safety number
   moves**, and their interface says *a device was added by Ana*.

---

## The rule

> Either the route is one an attacker cannot write to, **or** it carries a
> confirmation a person checks.

Never neither. That is the whole of it.

**This was written twice before it was right, and both wrong versions are worth
keeping.** The first required the two devices to meet in person, which made the
safety rest on the channel, so the channel had to be the strongest one every
time and anybody without a camera or without both devices to hand was left with
nothing.

The second over-corrected and demanded the confirmation on every route including
the scanned one. That is a step that buys nothing there: if the new device
*displays* its key package and the first device *reads it with a camera*, there
is no channel in between for anybody to substitute anything in. The digits would
be compared against the same screen that just showed the code. Charging a person
for a check that cannot fail is how a check becomes something people learn to
click through, which is how it stops working on the routes where it matters.

The substitution being defended against is a swapped key package. A screen read
directly by a camera prevents one. A confirmation derived from the bytes that
arrived **detects** one, whatever carried them, because an attacker who
substitutes the package changes what the receiving end computes and the two
screens stop agreeing.

Prevention or detection. Either is enough; neither is not.

### The routes

| Route | When it suits | How the package is authenticated |
|---|---|---|
| **The new device shows a code, the first device's camera reads it** | The usual case: the desktop displays, the phone scans | By the route. Nothing to compare |
| **A short string typed from one device into the other** | No camera, or one that will not focus | The confirmation |
| **Through the mailbox** | The two devices are not in the same place | The confirmation |
| **Read aloud** | Anything above is impractical | The confirmation |

**The desktop displays and the phone reads, not the other way round.** The phone
is already in a hand and has the better camera; reversing it demands a webcam on
the desktop and somebody holding a phone up to it.

The last row is not a joke and it is not a lesser option. Two devices belonging
to one person are usually in one pair of hands, and a person reading four words
off one screen and checking them on the other is doing exactly what the first
row does, more slowly and with no camera.

### What the confirmation has to be

Short enough to be compared without resentment and long enough that guessing it
is not worth trying. The same reasoning as the safety number, and it should be
generated by the same code so there is one construction to review rather than
two.

Derived from the key package **as received**, never from the one that was meant
to arrive. That is the whole mechanism, and it is the sentence to check any
future change against.

### Where a route may not be weakened

A route that satisfies **neither** half of the rule is refused, whatever it is.
The shape to watch for is "approve on your other device", where a package
arrived over something an attacker could write to and a person is asked to tap
yes with nothing in front of them to compare. That tap means "yes, whatever that
was", and what it grants is membership of every conversation the person has.

A scanned code is not that, because nothing could have been substituted. A typed
string without its digits is.

## What the partner is told, and why the wording matters

The credential says which person a device claims to belong to, and **a
credential is only ever a claim**. What makes the claim worth anything is who
committed the Add: a device is in the group because a member already in it put it
there.

So the interface says *a device was added by Ana*, not *Ana added a device*. The
first is what the group actually knows. The second asserts that the person, and
not something holding the person's key, made a decision.

And the safety number moves. That is the point of having one: a device nobody
mentioned shows up as different digits the next time two people compare.

---

## What a second device does not get

**The history.** Forward secrecy means nobody keeps the material to rebuild a
conversation, so there is nothing to hand over and no honest way to invent it. A
new device starts from the moment it joined. Handing a copy over deliberately,
with the group told, is a separate thing and is item 3 of
[`USABILITY-PLAN.md`](USABILITY-PLAN.md).

**Any authority the first device has.** Both are members. Either can add or
remove, including removing the other, and every one of those is a commit
everybody sees. There is no primary device, because a primary device is a thing
that can be lost.

---

## The alternatives, and why each is refused

**Copy the sealed session to the second device.** The simplest, and it is the
defect this project already paid for. Two copies of one session are two clones
spending the same generations, which the receiver refuses, and the rekey that
answers that is what left two phones at two epochs neither could leave. See §5b
of the threat model. It is not a shortcut to the design above, it is the bug.

**A long-lived person key that signs each device key.** It would make a device
verifiable without meeting, and it is exactly the identifier this project does
not have: one value, stable across every conversation, that ties them together
for anybody who sees two of them. §4 of the threat model refuses it and this does
not get to be the exception.

**A server that holds the device list.** Then the mailbox knows a person exists,
how many devices they have, and when they add one. It knows none of those now.

**Adding a device without telling the partners.** This is the ghost-member
attack with better manners. Every addition is a commit and stays one.

---

## Where this can be stopped

After steps 1 to 3 exist and can be tested between two processes, with no
conversation yet joined. That is two devices agreeing on a key package and on
the confirmation over it, which is the whole of the security, with nothing in
any existing conversation changed.

Worth testing there rather than later: that a **substituted key package makes
the two confirmations differ**. A test that only shows two honest devices
agreeing would pass with the confirmation computed over the wrong bytes, which
is the one mistake that would quietly remove the protection while leaving every
route working.
